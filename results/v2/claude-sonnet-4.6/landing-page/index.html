On the work

A pressed sheet holds
the weight of the hand
that made it.

My father learned to pull paper in a workshop without windows — he said the light inside the vat told you everything. I learned it from him, knee-deep in a cold river, understanding that kozo fiber carries the story of the tree it came from.

Every commission begins not with design but with touch. We ask what the object must feel like before we discuss what it must say. Weight, surface, transparency, deckle — these are the first vocabulary, and they determine everything that follows.

We keep our press small by intention. Four craftspeople, one press, a drying room that smells of rice paste and cold iron. We print twelve to thirty projects each year. We do not rush.

How a sheet comes into the world

一

Fiber preparation

Kozo or gampi bark is cooked in wood ash lye, then beaten by hand until the fiber reaches the desired length — shorter for translucency, longer for strength.

二

Vat and formation

Fiber is suspended in cold water with neri, a mucilage that slows drainage and allows the thin layer to build. The mould is moved in long, deliberate waves.

三

Couching and pressing

Each wet sheet is laid onto dampened felts and pressed overnight. Twelve hours. The weight does not vary.

四

Drying on cedar board

Sheets are brushed onto seasoned cedar boards and left in the open air. The drying is supervised — a single day of wind can change the surface character entirely.

五

Impression and aging

For letterpress work, dry sheets are humidified to 62% and printed on our 1964 Heidelberg Tiegel. The finished piece rests for a minimum of fourteen days before delivery.
